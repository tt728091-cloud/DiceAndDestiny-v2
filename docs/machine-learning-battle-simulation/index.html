Executive direction

Two phases, one controlled experiment

First make the Go battle authority support two equal local controllers. Then attach the smallest credible learning system to that exact interface. Engine correctness comes before model training.

01

Seat A and Seat B each control an existing Blade Warden through the full authoritative battle flow. A human driver, script, heuristic, or future model can occupy either seat.

Server-firstNo networkingExisting content

Machine-learning mirror pilot

Two policy instances play Blade Warden mirror matches repeatedly. Results test learning, seat symmetry, rules integrity, and experiment reproducibility—not roster balance by themselves.

Controller architecture

Separate the creature from who makes its decisions

A combatant definition owns cards, dice, health, resources, abilities, and statuses. A controller owns decision selection for a runtime seat. Every controller submits ordinary legal commands; only the authority changes battle state.

ControllerRole
ExternalA local human driver, test script, or model receives observations and submits actions.
D100Preserves current Venom Goblin PvE behavior as a baseline and fallback.
Random legalValidates complete action enumeration and establishes the minimum-skill benchmark.
HeuristicProvides a transparent and debuggable competence baseline before neural training.
Learned policyScores the legal actions exposed by the authority and returns one candidate index.

Phase 1

Build the local two-seat authority path

Phase 1 proves that two peer controllers can complete a real Blade Warden mirror match on one computer. A focused server-level driver is more useful here than a polished Godot hot-seat screen because the immediate consumer is the learning environment.

1

Decouple combatant and controller

Assign an existing creature to an external seat independently of its authored default controller.

2

Create symmetric setup

Seat A and Seat B are peers with separate Blade Warden instances, cards, dice, and private viewer identities.

3

Replace the single-human assumption

Track pending input by actor or required-actor set, and stop automatic progression whenever either external seat must choose.

4

Protect hidden simultaneous planning

Neither seat sees the opponent's hidden offensive commitment before both commitments are complete.

5

Define reaction priority

Specify first opportunity, passing, round completion, and the measurement of possible seat advantage.

6

Enumerate complete legal actions

Return submission-ready commands with current IDs, targets, dice subsets, card instances, and required payload fields.

7

Provide a local two-seat driver

A CLI or focused local tool can inspect each viewer-safe state and submit a legal choice. No network service is needed.

8

Protect existing behavior

Keep authority, recovery, snapshots, deterministic battles, the current D100 path, and Godot verification passing.

Environment operations established in Phase 1

Reset

Create a seeded Blade Warden mirror battle from current content.

Observe seat

Return only information legally visible to the selected seat.

List legal actions

Return complete command candidates for the actor who owns input.

Submit

Validate and apply one candidate through the normal authority path.

Advance

Resolve automatic work until another decision or the terminal state.

Record

Capture seed, versions, actions, outcome, and replay identifiers.

Phase 1 acceptance criteria

  • Two distinct externally controlled Blade Wardens can start a battle.
  • Neither equal seat uses the D100 offensive shortcut.
  • Each seat receives an independent viewer-safe observation.
  • Hidden planning information remains hidden until reveal.
  • Every pending input exposes a complete legal action.
  • Stale, wrong-seat, and fabricated commands are rejected.
  • Reaction priority is explicit, deterministic, and tested.
  • A scripted mirror battle reaches a valid terminal result.
  • A developer can manually drive both seats without Godot.
  • Standard Go and Godot authority checks still pass.

Phase 2

Run the first machine-learning mirror pilot

Use a small reinforcement-learning policy, not an LLM. Battle observations and actions are structured, so an LLM would add latency, packaging cost, and legal-action risk without a matching benefit. Maskable PPO is a sensible first algorithm only after random and heuristic validation.

ElementFirst-pilot decision
EnvironmentA long-running local Go simulator using the real engine; no Godot process or subprocess launch per action.
ObservationViewer-safe battle state plus necessary public history; no hidden-card or hidden-commitment leakage.
ActionAn index into a padded list of complete legal command candidates, with invalid slots masked.
Reward+1 victory, −1 defeat, 0 draw. Add shaping only after proving it preserves the intended objective.
Episode endAuthority-reported terminal status, plus a defined safety truncation for defective infinite episodes.
RandomnessReproducible episode seeds with content, environment, and model versions recorded.
ParallelismMultiple independent local environments, with worker count selected by measurement.

Build and training sequence

  1. Validate random legal play. Large batches finish without crashes, illegal commands, stale input, or nonterminating battles.
  2. Add a transparent heuristic. Establish a debuggable baseline stronger than random.
  3. Wrap vectorized environments. Batch observations, masks, rewards, terminal flags, and diagnostics.
  4. Train from several seeds. A single successful run is not reliable evidence.
  5. Evaluate out of sample. Hold back battle seeds and compare both seat assignments.
  6. Replay surprising battles. Record commands so diagnosis never depends on identical floating-point inference.

First experiment ladder

ExperimentPurposeMinimum output
Random vs randomEnvironment and seat-symmetry smoke testCompletion, invalid actions, seat win rate, duration
Heuristic vs randomVerify that better decisions create measurable advantageWin rate, confidence interval, representative replays

Hardware: the M3 Max is a good computer for this pilot. Start CPU-first because Go simulation throughput is the major workload. Test Apple's MPS backend only if neural-network updates become the measured bottleneck. No special hardware is required.

Phase 2 acceptance criteria

  • Two model policy instances can occupy the two local seats.
  • Invalid action rate is zero outside deliberate negative tests.
  • Training is reproducible from versions, configuration, and seed.
  • At least three training seeds produce checkpoints and curves.
  • Evaluation includes random, heuristic, mirror, and held-out tests.
  • Initial report uses at least 1,000 evaluation battles.
  • Ambiguous findings can be confirmed with 10,000 or adaptive games.
  • No balance claim relies on mirror convergence alone.

Interpretation

What the first results can honestly tell us

An identical-policy Blade Warden mirror match is primarily a test of seat symmetry, controller strength, experiment stability, and rules integrity. A result near 50% does not establish roster balance because only one creature is present.

Win rate

Detect Seat A/Seat B advantage and compare policy strength.

Draws

Reveal stalemates, truncations, and missing terminal rules.

Duration

Measure pace and identify policies that learn to delay.

Health margin

Capture decisiveness, not just binary outcomes.

Damage

Separate offensive output from prevention and recovery.

Action mix

Expose dominant cards, abilities, rerolls, or passing.

Dead turns

Find nonproductive behavior and possible reward problems.

Seed sensitivity

Separate stable strategy from lucky training runs.

Latency

Confirm simulation and inference remain practical.

Confidence

Report uncertainty instead of treating win rate as exact.

Statistical guide: around 1,000 games gives a worst-case 95% sampling margin of roughly ±3.1 percentage points near a true 50% win rate. Around 10,000 games reduces it to roughly ±1 point.

Implementation order

Work from engine contract to proof

  1. Audit the current asymmetry. Trace participant assembly, human-actor discovery, pending-input ownership, automatic AI resolution, viewer filtering, and the existing deterministic full-battle test. Report any material conflict with this plan before expanding scope.
  2. Introduce controller-neutral seat ownership. An existing combatant can use an external controller without changing its authored content definition or breaking the D100 controller.
  3. Support two peer external actors. Battle setup, pending input, and automatic progression must handle either seat—and any rule window that requires commitments from both.
  4. Preserve hidden information and define priority. Prevent commitment leakage, formalize reaction priority/pass behavior, and test both seat assignments.
  5. Enumerate submission-ready legal actions. Each candidate must contain the current IDs, targets, dice/card instances, checkpoints, and payload required for immediate authority submission.
  6. Add a local developer driver. A focused server-side CLI or test driver can observe and command both seats without networking or a Godot UI.
  7. Prove the complete mirror battle. Add deterministic tests in which two external Blade Wardens complete the normal rules path to victory, defeat, or draw.
  8. Verify and stop. Run the required Go and Godot checks, summarize changes and risks, and wait for owner review before Phase 2.

Deferred ideas

Future balance lab & production AI

These directions are valuable, but none is required to complete the local two-controller battle or first Blade Warden learning pilot. Each item has a trigger for deliberate reconsideration.

Universal roster policy

Hundreds of separate creature models will not scale. A future policy should understand structured mechanics—cards, dice faces, costs, qualifications, statuses, health, resources, elements, and defenses—rather than memorize only creature IDs.

Revisit when: at least three mechanically contrasting creatures exist and the pilot schemas are stable.

Population self-play

Two current models can develop shared blind spots. A stronger population mixes current policies, old checkpoints, heuristics, difficulty policies, search agents, and targeted best responses.

Revisit when: the first policy reliably beats random and competes with the heuristic across seeds.

Search & expert distillation

Cloneable battle state may support Monte Carlo search or MCTS as a development-time expert. Expensive search behavior could later be distilled into a small, fast policy for shipping.

Revisit when: legal actions and cloned simulation branches reproduce authoritative results.

Balance beyond mirrors

Mirror matches test seat symmetry, not roster strength. Future evidence should combine cross-matchup matrices, policy-strength ladders, targeted best responses, pace metrics, and human playtests.

Revisit when: at least three completed creatures and a stable expert policy pool exist.

Adaptive statistical sampling

A 300-creature roster creates 44,850 unordered pairings before levels, builds, seats, and policy strengths. Future evaluation should emphasize benchmark suites, ratings, uncertainty, and additional games near decision boundaries.

Revisit when: evaluation expands beyond a small hand-curated matchup set.

Production enemy AI

A learned policy can eventually replace or complement D100. Ship a small candidate-scoring model, keep authority validation, record chosen commands for replays, pin every schema version, and retain D100 or heuristic fallback.

Revisit when: learned play clearly exceeds the intended PvE baseline and is enjoyable for players.

Difficulty & personality

The optimal bot is not automatically the most enjoyable. Difficulty can use different checkpoints, controlled action sampling, deliberate mistakes, restricted information, or bounded search. Personality can bias legal preferences.

Revisit when: the policy is being judged as a player-facing opponent.

Hardware & cloud scaling

The M3 Max is enough to begin. Cloud compute becomes useful for large sweeps, huge experience budgets, wide policy populations, or broad roster evaluation—not before local profiling proves a bottleneck.

Revisit when: measured compute time, rather than correctness or experiment design, limits progress.

Teams and evolving mechanics

Teams, switching, benches, elements, progression, evolution, and new content operations enlarge observations and actions. Add them only after versioned environment contracts exist.

Revisit when: each mechanic has settled deterministic server rules and focused authority tests.

Actual network multiplayer

Local controller neutrality is a useful foundation, but online PvP is a separate product: identity, sessions, transport, reconnects, timeouts, anti-cheat, compatibility, and player UX.

Revisit when: there is an explicit player-facing online PvP goal independent of ML balance work.
