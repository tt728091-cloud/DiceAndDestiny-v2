Status: This is a design discussion record, not a claim that every feature is implemented. Example values such as deck sizes and level caps remain hypotheses until playtesting supports them.

Current design center: The player owns many creatures, selects an active team of four, and deploys one at a time. Each creature is a self-contained deck-as-health combatant with its own cards, dice, abilities, statuses, progression, and elemental identity.

How to read this document

This document intentionally distinguishes settled foundations from provisional design ideas. Numbers such as a 46-card final deck or level 50 are useful working examples, not approved production limits.

1. Product north star

WORKING: Dice and Destiny is becoming a battle-centered creature-collection game in which the player's strategic expression comes from selecting, developing, and sequencing complete creature builds.

Core experience

  • Battles remain the central gameplay event and the most deeply developed part of the game.
  • The player finds, defeats, recruits, and owns many creatures over time.
  • A lightweight route or event layer brings the player to battles, shops, discoveries, risks, and upgrade opportunities.
  • Cards, dice, abilities, statuses, elements, equipment, and switching create the tactical language of combat.
  • Progression should make the player feel that a powerful result was earned through preparation and synergy, not handed out as a flat numerical bonus.

Run-level engine building

The roguelike transcript discussed in this thread provides a useful design lens even if Dice and Destiny is not ultimately labeled a roguelike. It separates actions that advance the objective from actions that strengthen the engine used to advance it.

Action classDice and Destiny examplesDesign purpose
AdvanceBattle, boss, story encounter, milestoneMoves the run or story toward completion.
BuildRecruitment, shop, card upgrade, die modification, ability developmentImproves the creature team and creates future combinations.

The run should offer frequent build-upgrade moments, including a variable number of optional opportunities. Taking additional encounters, rerolling a shop, pursuing an elite fight, or remaining in an area longer should offer more chances to strengthen the team while increasing danger, resource cost, or time pressure.

Design principles imported from the transcript

  • Enumerate the meaningful actions players can deliberately perform, then create multiple rewards that make different actions strategically valuable.
  • Favor synergy parts over universally optimal upgrades. An upgrade should be excellent in the right build rather than mandatory in every build.
  • Provide enough upgrade decisions that the final build feels authored by the player.
  • Give players controlled ways to spend resources to reduce bad randomness, adapt after bad luck, or deliberately accept more risk.
  • Keep early runs approachable while allowing expert players to extract more value through optional danger and efficient preparation.
  • Create variety at the beginning of a new run through different creatures, starting teams, traits, or initial strategic identities.

2. Existing battle foundation

DECIDED / IMPLEMENTED FOUNDATION: Damage is represented by permanent card removal during battle. Current health is the total number of cards remaining across deck, discard, and hand.

The existing authority design already models reusable YAML definitions for cards, dice, statuses, abilities, and combatants. Definitions are immutable content; runtime instances carry mutable zones, rolls, stacks, ownership, and battle state.

FoundationCurrent meaning
Deck-as-healthCurrent health equals deck + discard + hand. Removed cards represent damage or missing health.
Combat sheetAn actor references its decklist, dice loadout, abilities, starting resources, and statuses.
CardsDrawn, played, discarded, removed, and potentially recovered through future healing rules.
AbilitiesActor actions separate from cards; offensive and defensive timing belongs to the ability definition.
DiceReusable die definitions with authored faces and symbols; the current default loadout is five D6.
StatusesReusable triggered or activated effects. Entangle already exists as a target design example.

The creature system should extend this foundation instead of introducing a parallel health model or a second incompatible content architecture.

3. Creature collection and team model

Collection and active team

DECIDED: The player may own a large collection, chooses an active team of four, and normally controls one deployed creature at a time.
  • Inactive collection: creatures owned outside the current team.
  • Active team: four creatures selected before entering an encounter or run segment.
  • Deployed creature: the one creature whose deck, dice, resources, abilities, and statuses are currently active.
  • Bench: the other three active-team creatures, preserving their individual battle state until deployed.

Creature, not commander, is the combatant

A separate player health bar is not currently needed. Each creature owns its own health through its own deck. If a creature is defeated, the player deploys another eligible team member. The battle is lost when the full active team can no longer continue.

Recruitment

WORKING: Defeating or resolving an encounter with a creature may create an opportunity to recruit that individual, but recruitment should be expressed through an original pact, bonding, rescue, negotiation, or post-battle dice/card procedure rather than a copied ball-throwing presentation.

Recruitment should usually compete with another valuable reward. A post-encounter choice might offer the creature, immediate upgrade materials, knowledge of one of its techniques, reputation, or a riskier second-stage challenge. This prevents recruiting every creature from becoming an automatic decision.

4. Independent creature combat sheets

DECIDED: There is no persistent command deck mixed into every creature. Each creature owns a complete, internally coherent deck.

The shared-deck concept was rejected because cards are simultaneously abilities and health. Mixing player command cards with creature cards would make health ownership ambiguous, dilute creature-specific synergies, and make switching difficult to reason about.

Team synergy without deck mixing

Team composition can matter through the battlefield rather than by shuffling all four decks together.

  • A creature applies a status that another creature exploits.
  • A recalled creature leaves Armor, weather, terrain, a hazard, or a healing field.
  • A creature has a tag-in, tag-out, or bench passive.
  • The incoming creature inherits, consumes, or transforms an existing effect.
  • A creature becomes stronger when an ally is defeated or when a particular element preceded it.

Switching state

WORKING: When a creature leaves play, its remaining deck-health, discard, removed cards, resources, and persistent statuses remain attached to that creature.

A simple prototype rule is that switching consumes the active creature's turn, its current hand moves to its own discard pile, and the incoming creature draws from its own deck. This avoids storing four prepared perfect hands. The cost, status timing, and special switching abilities remain open for testing.

5. Deck-as-health implications

Health ownership

Only the active creature normally receives direct damage. Unblocked damage proposes and then removes occurrences from that creature's own hand, deck, or discard according to the existing damage authority rules. Bench damage may exist later as an explicit special effect.

Deck size is both survivability and content

Adding a card increases maximum health and adds a tactical option. Upgrading a card increases quality without increasing health. Those are meaningfully different level rewards and should not be assumed equal without testing.

BALANCE WARNING: A larger deck can be healthier but less consistent. More cards dilute access to key engine pieces unless additions include useful duplicates, draw control, search, predictable opening cards, or broader redundant synergies.

Working scale question

A final 46-card deck and a Level 1 12-card deck are current thought experiments, not commitments. With a five-card draw, a 46-card first cycle lasts more than nine turns. That may be appropriate if damage steadily removes cards, but it must be tested against target battle length, reshuffle behavior, healing, and draw consistency.

6. Attack and counter-defense model

WORKING: Use the useful separation found in Pokémon - power, category, type, reliability, resource limit, and secondary effect - while resolving defense through Dice and Destiny counter rolls instead of an automatic defense divisor.

Proposed resolution pipeline

  1. The card or ability establishes potential attack power.
  2. Attacker dice, symbols, resources, and bonuses determine incoming damage.
  3. Attack category selects the applicable defensive response.
  4. Elemental advantage or resistance modifies one agreed part of the interaction.
  5. The defender rolls its counter dice or resolves a defensive ability.
  6. Each successful save prevents one point of incoming damage.
  7. Unblocked damage removes cards from the active creature through the authoritative damage flow.
  8. Secondary statuses or follow-up effects resolve from explicit triggers.

Defense rolls should replace the defense portion of a Pokémon-like formula, not follow an earlier Defense reduction. Otherwise Defense would be counted twice.

Defense probability

For ordinary D6 saves, the baseline expected prevention is:

Formula: Expected blocks = number of dice × successful faces ÷ six.
Defense profileSave chanceExpected blocksPossible blocksPlay feel
10D6, save on 4-650%50-10High capacity and greater variance; can theoretically stop everything.
6D6, save on 2-683.33%50-6Reliable but capped; against 10 damage at least 4 gets through.

These profiles have the same average prevention but create different defensive personalities. The system can separately tune capacity, reliability, expected prevention, and variance.

Accuracy caution

If an action first rolls to hit, then rolls attack dice, then allows a defense counter roll, too many turns may accomplish nothing. The preferred initial experiment is for ordinary attacks to connect, attacker dice to establish strength, and defender dice to supply uncertainty. Complete miss chances can be reserved for unusually powerful attacks.

Elemental modifier caution

Elemental advantage should modify one layer rather than increasing damage, reducing defense, and improving accuracy simultaneously. A first prototype could add or remove counter dice instead of using Pokémon's large ×2 multipliers.

7. Levels, evolution, and backwards progression

WORKING: Create and balance the creature's complete end-game state first. Once the final engine is enjoyable, walk it backwards into evolution stages and a functional Level 1 core.

Why end-state-first helps

  • It proves the creature has a distinctive and enjoyable completed identity.
  • It exposes whether the final deck size, dice, abilities, and statuses are manageable.
  • It lets designers classify every component as essential core, growth, upgrade, or evolution milestone.
  • It makes level progression a controlled reveal of a known system rather than improvising power indefinitely.

Minimum viable engine at Level 1

Walking backwards must preserve the central loop. A plant creature cannot wait until Level 20 to receive the payoff for Entangle if its early deck already applies Entangle. Its Level 1 deck should contain a small enabler, payoff, attack, defense, and dice/resource loop.

8. Elements, dice, abilities, and statuses

Elements

WORKING: Creatures should have levels and elemental identities. Begin with a small element set and one element per creature; delay dual elements until the single-element system is proven.

Elements should organize mechanics already present rather than become a disconnected damage chart. A plant-aligned creature might use growth, recovery, poison, Entangle, and long-horizon value. A stone-aligned creature might favor shields and reliable prevention. Exact element names and matchups remain open.

Dice

Dice progression should be milestone-based rather than automatic every level. Possible changes include adding a die, replacing a blank face, adding an elemental symbol, strengthening a symbol, unlocking a reroll, or allowing a stored result. Dice improvements primarily govern consistency and access, not raw health.

Abilities

Abilities are not cards in the current combat-sheet model. They can become stronger, gain additional status effects, or transform at evolution milestones. A basic vine action might later gain Entangle, consume Entangle for a payoff, or alter defensive dice without being shuffled into the deck.

Statuses

Statuses are a major source of creature and team identity. Existing target content already defines Entangle as reducing the victim's maximum Offensive rolls at the next relevant trigger. Creature designs should compose generic status operations rather than branch on a specific creature or content ID.

9. Lessons from Pokémon Red/Blue

RESEARCH: Pokémon is a light structural reference, not a mathematical or expressive template to reproduce.

What Generation I actually did

  • Species had predetermined natural level-up learnsets.
  • Players still chose which four moves to retain and whether to spend single-use TMs or use HMs.
  • Moves did not normally level up themselves; damage increased because level and offensive stats increased, while stronger moves could be learned later.
  • Evolution was the largest visible transformation, changing species base stats, appearance, and sometimes typing or learnset behavior.
  • Generation I did not have the later passive Ability system.

Move anatomy

A move's type is independent of the attacker's type. A Psychic creature using an Ice move launches an Ice attack only. Matching the creature's element may grant an affinity bonus; the target's element determines advantage or resistance.

Power is not final damage. Pokémon combines move power with level, the relevant attacking and defensive stats, same-type bonus, type effectiveness, randomness, critical hits, and other modifiers. Dice and Destiny can preserve the separation of concerns while replacing the automatic defensive calculation with counter rolls.

HP research

Generation I HP used species Base HP, level, an individual DV, and accumulated Stat Experience. Bulbasaur's Base HP was 45, but its actual HP varied by individual and training. A level-100 Bulbasaur could reach hundreds of numerical HP because Pokémon's damage scale was built around that range.

Important: Pokémon's 293-HP upper example does not imply a 293-card deck. Numerical health is meaningful only relative to the game's attack scale. Dice and Destiny can represent equivalent battle duration with 12, 20, 46, or another number of cards if damage and prevention are calibrated to match.

Ability-pattern library

The useful research output is a library of abstract action patterns, not copied names or exact data. Examples include reliable low power plus debuff, powerful low-accuracy attack, multi-hit, drain, recoil, charge, counterattack, escalating repeated use, recovery, and status payoff.

10. Legal and originality guardrails

This section records general design guidance, not legal advice.

Generally reusable ideas

  • Collecting creatures, selecting a smaller active team, deploying one at a time, leveling, evolution, elemental matchups, turn-based battles, cards, dice, and status effects are broad game concepts.
  • US copyright guidance says game ideas, systems, mathematical formulas, algorithms, and methods of play are not protected by copyright, although original artwork, writing, code, and other expression can be protected.

Areas requiring originality or review

  • Creature artwork, silhouettes, names, lore, animations, sounds, UI, terminology, world, and marketing should be independently created.
  • Do not copy Pokémon's source code, move text, creature data, names, art, capture presentation, or overall commercial identity.
  • Patents are separate from copyright and can cover specific implementations. Recruitment, aimed capture objects, summoning, riding, or other mechanically specific systems should be reviewed before release if they resemble active patent claims.
  • Conduct trademark clearance for the game name and major branding before public launch.

Palworld clarification

Nintendo and The Pokémon Company sued Pocketpair in Japan for alleged patent infringement, not a copyright claim that the creatures merely looked similar. As of this document date, the case had not produced a final public judgment. Pocketpair changed sphere-based summoning and glider-Pal behavior while continuing to contest infringement and validity.

11. Prototype and validation plan

RECOMMENDATION: Do not author dozens of progression curves yet. First prove a small set of complete end-game creatures and the shared combat measurements.

Phase A - lock the measurement system

  1. Define the target deck-health range and target battle duration.
  2. Define how incoming damage is created from cards, abilities, and attacker dice.
  3. Choose the first defensive model: current defensive abilities only, Guard/Ward profiles, or another explicit counter structure.
  4. Build a probability calculator for damage, prevention, variance, and full-block outcomes.
  5. Choose one elemental modifier and avoid counting advantage in multiple layers.

Phase B - build final-state creatures

  1. Create one plant/Entangle creature with a complete final deck, dice, abilities, and passive.
  2. Create at least two contrasting final creatures, such as a reliable armored defender and a volatile aggressive creature.
  3. Run standardized matchups repeatedly and measure draw consistency, damage, prevention, battle length, and dead turns.
  4. Adjust final deck sizes and repeated card counts until each complete engine is coherent.

Phase C - walk backwards

  1. Mark every final card as core, addition, upgrade, evolution-gated, or optional variant.
  2. Construct a functional 12-card Level 1 engine.
  3. Create milestone snapshots before filling every level: Level 1, first evolution, middle game, second evolution, and final level.
  4. Test each snapshot against level-appropriate benchmarks.
  5. Only then fill the exact per-level addition and upgrade schedule.

Phase D - connect the run layer

  1. Prototype recruitment as a post-encounter choice competing with another valuable reward.
  2. Prototype a lightweight route containing battles, shops, risks, recovery, and optional build-upgrade opportunities.
  3. Measure whether players can deliberately pursue a strategy, recover from poor offers, and choose between progress and preparation.
